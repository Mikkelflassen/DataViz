        Pick a country in the list or click it on the map. Every other country is coloured by its favoritism: how many more or fewer points it gives than expected.

        Expected is the average the receiving country got from all voters that year. +10 means about ten extra points in every final the two met in. A song that everyone liked raises the average too, so it does not show up as favoritism.

        Who favours it colours the countries that give points to the selected country. Who it favours colours the countries it gives points to.

        The page opens on the country you are visiting from (we guess from the browser's time zone and language). Add #dk, #se and so on to the address to open on another one.

        Only finals count, 1957–2023. There was no contest in 2020, and the 2023 "rest of world" televote is left out.

        A pair has to have met often enough for its average to mean something: at least 5 shared finals for ranges of 20 years or more, 3 for 8–19 years and 1 for shorter ranges. Pairs below that are hatched.

        The presets follow the voting rules: the 12-point system up to 2003, semi-finals from 2004, separate jury and televote points from 2016.

        The colours use a symmetric log scale (symlog). Half of all country pairs are within ±1 point and a few reach +13. On a straight scale the small ones all turn grey.

        Symlog is linear close to 0 and logarithmic further out, so you can tell 0.5 from 2 and also 5 from 13. A plain log scale would not work, because the values can be 0 or negative.

        Both sides use the same steps: equally strong red and blue mean the same number of points.

        Andorra, Monaco, San Marino, Malta and Luxembourg are too small to click, so they are drawn as dots.

        Australia, Yugoslavia and Serbia & Montenegro sit in the boxes on the left. Grey countries never took part. Hover a country for its exact value.

        The teal shapes join the pairs of countries that favour each other most, ranked by the average of the two directions.

        Each pair has two wedges, one per direction. A wedge is wide at the country that gives the points and narrows to a point at the country that receives them. The wider the base, the more extra points go that way. When a friendship is one-sided, one wedge is clearly thinner than the other.

        We used arrows here first. With several lines meeting in the Balkans the arrowheads piled up, and readers find direction faster on tapered lines than on arrows (Holten & van Wijk 2009).

        Hover a wedge or a name in the list to pick out one pair. With … shows only the selected country's bonds. Slide to 0 to hide them.
